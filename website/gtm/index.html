The launch strategy

Positioning, audience, channels, measurement, and the first 30 days.

Prepared 21 September 2026. Budget: founder-led organic growth, with little or no paid spend. This is a strategy and content proposal. Campaign posts and outreach have not been sent; the assets below are drafts.

Launch the existing Linty app to a narrow audience, learn whether they keep using it, and use that evidence to expand. Start with developers and indie builders on Apple Silicon who regularly write prompts, issue descriptions, and project updates. Keep the core app free during this experiment. A separate Lite app would split attention before we know what prevents adoption.

The initial positioning to test is: “Turn spoken thoughts into text, right where you work. Free, on-device dictation for your Mac.” For developer channels, lead with “Speak your next prompt.” Follow with the actual workflow: choose a language, hold a key, speak, release to paste. The supporting reasons to try Linty are local speech recognition, optional local English cleanup, no account or API key, and free downloadable software with MIT-licensed application source.

This is a credible offer, not a claim that Linty invented the category. There is no exclusive, defensible feature advantage established by this research. Handy, FluidVoice, and OpenWhispr already compete strongly on free local dictation; several paid products already target developers. Linty can earn preference through a focused, dependable experience, useful support, and publicly demonstrated results. “Simpler” and “more reliable” are hypotheses to test, not claims to publish yet. See the competitor research.

The current baseline changes what we should optimize.

The public GitHub API reported 2 stars and 0 forks during this research. Across all 61 release records returned, assets had 1,329 downloads: 67 DMG downloads, 80 updater archive downloads, and 1,182 other asset downloads. The latest release was v0.0.67; its DMG was 12,899,522 bytes, approximately 12.9 MB. These are request counters, not unique people, completed installations, or retained users. The current all-assets badge should not become “1,329 users” in marketing. Repository metadata, release records.

The live site responds and has a clear direct download path. Its hero describes general dictation, and the HTML inspected did not contain an og:image social-preview image. The README gives substantial space to engine comparisons and synthetic file benchmarks before installation. These are useful technical details, but a short real workflow demonstration and obvious installation path should appear earlier. The existing comparison also omits the closest free alternatives. Start with a reachable group who have a repeated job.

Recruit 20 Apple Silicon users, ideally 10 developers who already use dictation and 10 who write long prompts or updates but currently type. Include different Mac generations and English accents. Existing dictation users reveal switching costs; new users reveal whether onboarding teaches a useful habit. Select people with a place where speaking aloud is practical. Writers and general Mac productivity users are the next audience if this cohort retains.

Ask each participant to do three ordinary tasks: describe a bug, dictate a prompt or project brief, and write a short update. Observe the initial installation without coaching, then help after documenting the obstacle. Do not ask for private code, prompts, or recordings. The same synthetic sample can be used for a published demo; personal workflow outcomes can be reported without sharing content.

Record where they hesitate: permissions, model download, trigger key, text quality, paste destination, or remembering to use it tomorrow. At day 7 ask how many days they used it, whether it replaced typing or another product, and what made them stop. With 20 people, these findings are directional. Report counts and missing replies, not statistically conclusive percentages.

Make the first success easier before adding features.

English speech setup is about 500 MB plus roughly 496 MB for cleanup according to current app estimates. That makes first-run waiting more relevant than the DMG size. Measure total setup time, model-transfer time, and time from models-ready to first successful paste separately. If downloads are the main abandonment point, test allowing basic dictation before optional cleanup finishes. If paste reliability is the main problem, prioritize it ahead of new engines or another interface.

For the first 20-person cohort, use these proposed launch gates: at least 14 complete a first useful dictation without live help, at least 8 report use on 3 separate days in their first week, and no unresolved recurring loss-of-text issue appears. Review every unsuccessful attempt. These thresholds are management choices for the experiment, not industry benchmarks or launch guarantees.

Create a small set of proof-led assets.

Prioritize one actual workflow video, a focused download page, a useful README, three social creatives derived from the same footage, a founder launch post, one tutorial, and one comparison. A small companion tool is a later experiment.

The best candidate is a “Type or dictate?” challenge on linty.ai: a sample task, local timers, editable text boxes, and a manual correction-time field. Users dictate using the installed Linty app, compare total task time including correction, and can explicitly export a result card. This measures their own experience; it is not an independent benchmark. It should have no browser speech-recognition engine, no automatic transcript upload, and no misleading preset speedup. Keep it within the existing site/repository. Build it only if the demo does not sufficiently explain the benefit and at least five testers want the comparison. Start with a written challenge first.

A generic AI writing side-app or separate Lite installer would add maintenance and attract a less relevant audience. A browser transcription demo also would not prove system-wide Mac pasting. A second engine, new platform, or smaller model should follow observed adoption barriers, with accuracy and setup tradeoffs tested explicitly.

The next concrete decision is to run the cohort and record the demo.

Assign one owner to recruitment and follow-up, one to content and channel replies, and one to product fixes; one founder can hold all three roles with scheduled time blocks. Keep no-cost core dictation as the offer for this launch. Revisit sponsorship or paid support once repeat usage and maintenance needs are clearer. Publish only measured results and approved testimonials, and keep “dictation for Mac” beside the Linty name so first-time visitors do not mistake it for a code-linting tool.

The observable patterns suggest four routes into the market.

Paid dictation vendors use product demonstrations, trials, creator relationships, and repeat billing to support acquisition. Open-source projects make the runnable product, repository, and contribution path easy to reach. Broader transcription products attract people through specific jobs such as meetings or subtitle exports. Every can introduce Monologue to an existing audience and bundle it with other products. These are descriptions of visible mechanisms, not proof of how much growth each mechanism caused.

For Linty, the relevant combination is a free, easy-to-try app plus convincing workflow footage, founder participation, and useful public engineering work. Buying attention would be premature before knowing whether users return. Replicating a paid affiliate scheme makes little sense while Linty has no purchase to fund commissions.

Linty's strongest present proposition is a particular combination of choices.

It provides free signed/notarized downloads, local recognition and optional local English cleanup, language-based model preparation, local history, a dictionary, and MIT-licensed app source for Apple Silicon Macs. Those choices support a useful promise of accessible local dictation. They do not establish market exclusivity, superior accuracy, or superior onboarding. Linty documentation, model notices.

Against a cloud-dependent workflow, demonstrate offline dictation after setup. Against a paid packaged app, make the free download and absence of account setup clear. Against Handy, FluidVoice, and OpenWhispr, acknowledge the overlap and invite a workflow comparison. Against built-in dictation, demonstrate history, the dictionary, and English cleanup in a task the prospective user actually performs. If those benefits do not matter to a particular person, they are not the first target customer.

A credible comparison needs measurements beyond inference speed.

Linty's published “30-minute file in 4.97 seconds” is a warm Parakeet inference result on one M3 Pro using synthetic English. It excludes setup, model loading, recording, UI, cleanup, and paste. It is interesting technical evidence, but cannot support “fastest dictation app,” “instant first run,” or a claim about real-world accent accuracy. Methodology.

Before publishing performance comparisons, use a repeatable set of real, consented or explicitly synthetic tasks, state which, and test the same tasks across the selected products. Include current versions/settings, microphone, Mac hardware, cold and warm starts, release-to-paste time, failed insertions, and correction time. Report multiple runs, medians and variability. Compare cleaned text on preserved meaning and usable output, because word error alone can penalize deliberate formatting. Network conditions matter for cloud products. Do not announce a win until the experiment is complete.

Some search results require particular care.

Several comparison articles were competitor-owned or affiliate-supported, so they were used only for discovery, not as the basis for product facts. A FluidVoice-branded domain claimed official status, but the project metadata linked to altic.dev/fluid; this report uses the repository-linked destination. MacWhisper has dictation today even though some third-party summaries describe it as file-only. Old Monologue launch pricing differs from its current page. Verify prices and features again when publishing a comparison.

The practical research conclusion is to compete through a specific, proven user experience and distribution discipline. No evidence here justifies building a second app to create a supposed feature gap. The next research investment should be conversations and observed use by the first 20 target users.

First blog article draft

A little less typing: introducing Linty for Mac

Sometimes you already know what you want to say. The work is getting it into the text box: describing a bug, explaining a feature, writing an update, or capturing a thought before you lose it.

Linty is a free, open-source dictation app for Apple Silicon Macs. Hold a key, speak, and release. It turns your speech into text on your Mac and pastes the result where you're working.

Start with one ordinary task

You don't need to change your whole workflow to try dictation. Open a note and describe the next thing you need to do. Or explain a bug in plain language before editing the details. Start with a short paragraph, read the result, and make any corrections you need.

Linty keeps a local history so you can find and copy previous transcriptions. Its personal dictionary lets you add recurring names or terms. For English, on-device text cleanup can help organize the output; you can switch it off if you prefer the direct transcription.

What setup involves

Linty supports macOS 14 and later on Apple Silicon. Download the app, move it into Applications, grant Microphone and Accessibility permissions, and choose your dictation language. Linty prepares the appropriate speech model. English setup also prepares the cleanup model.

Those models are separate downloads. You need a connection for setup, and the app checks for updates. After the models are ready, speech recognition and cleanup work offline. No account or API key is required.

What local means

Linty does not upload your recordings or transcripts for speech recognition or cleanup. Your history stays on your Mac. The app you paste into may have its own syncing or cloud processing, so local dictation doesn't change how that app handles text.

Why another dictation app?

There are good alternatives, including Handy, FluidVoice, and paid tools with different workflows and platform support. Linty's focus is a straightforward Mac experience with a free download and open application source. We want to learn whether its everyday choices work for you, and improve the parts that don't.

We build on upstream work including Whisper, Parakeet, FluidAudio, and S1-mini. The repository documents their attribution and the project's current limitations.
